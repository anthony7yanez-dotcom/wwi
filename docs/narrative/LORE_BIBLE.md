## Public history — believable through Chapter V

The Aureate Synod teaches that the First Fire brought warmth, stability, and life to a lifeless world. Guardians were appointed to maintain its sacred light. Rulers and religious orders protected civilization for centuries. Darkness, crop failures, monsters, and lost settlements follow its weakening. Extinction is believed to mean the end of existence. Attempts to extinguish it therefore appear to threaten everyone.

This belief is reinforced by monuments, damaged inscriptions, rituals, personal experience, and sincere helpers. The danger is real. Characters can reasonably believe that saving the flame will save their families. The protagonist is an ordinary inhabitant who chooses to act, without a prophecy, hidden royal inheritance, or predetermined destiny.

Nhalis has fading forests, deteriorating kingdoms, empty sanctuaries, ruined civilizations, catacombs, and inhabited settlements struggling to survive. Everyday needs give the journey emotional weight: bread, medicine, firewood, letters, shelter, and returning home.

## Objective history — hidden until Chapter VI

Nhalis existed before the First Fire. The First Keepers constructed mechanisms, temples, channels, braziers, and a central flame to collect and redistribute the world's vitality. This network is the Cinder Engine. The Keepers used its concentrated power to prolong their lives, regulate warmth and resources, and rule civilizations.

Centuries of extraction exhausted ecosystems. Settlements became dependent on distributed warmth while other regions withered. The Keepers taught that the very system creating that dependency was the world's salvation. The alleged golden age is a distorted or incomplete history, not proof that all existence began with the flame.

The fire contains genuine power. Its power was taken from Nhalis rather than freely given to it. Failing machinery and exhausted sources now cause genuine disasters in dependent regions. Abruptly extinguishing the network may kill people who depend on it. Continuing extraction perpetuates exploitation. The central conflict must respect both realities.

Chapter I's sanctuary is a local component of this network. Restoring it genuinely helps the courtyard, but the origin of that additional energy is never explained. The yielding inscription stays ambiguous religious poetry at this stage.

Rekindling in Chapter V fully restores the Engine. Warmth and light return selectively, while destructive extraction resumes elsewhere. Public celebration is sincere, and the protagonist's courage remains real. Their action is manipulated by an older ruling power; the ending must not dismiss them as foolish.

Chapter VI exposes the machinery through previously inaccessible evidence and changed familiar places. The party interrupts renewed extraction, destabilizing rather than fully destroying the system. The protagonist disappears amid collapsing light and machinery. A personal possession remains; a faint signal or unexplained ember suggests continuation. Do not confirm death or survival. A surviving Keeper recognizes that control extends beyond the discovered chamber. Do not explain every Keeper or exhaust the setting's mysteries.

## Factions and boundaries of knowledge

| Group | Public role / belief | Actual knowledge | Early presentation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary inhabitants | Sacred warmth keeps civilization alive | Personal losses and local religious teaching | Fear, skepticism, faith, practical kindness; no secret-history certainty |
| Ordinary Synod members | Protect roads, tend shrines, preserve life | Ritual practice and official records | Many sincerely help civilians; avoid universal corruption |
| Synod leadership | Preserve the First Fire | Unequal access to censored records and Engine secrets; highest authorities serve Keepers | Credible assistance and authority, not obvious early villainy |
| First Keepers | Ancient appointed guardians in official history | Architects and beneficiaries of the Engine | Influence through law, architecture, seals, and sacred institutions; identities unresolved |
| Ashen Covenant reformers | Publicly branded darkness cultists | Suppressed historical fragments; belief that flame imprisons the world | May protect refugees or expose abuses; cannot prove the complete mechanism early |
| Covenant extremists | Break seals and summon outside entities | Partial ideological knowledge, dangerous ritual techniques | Responsible for real harm; liberation claims never erase victims |
| Independent scholars | Preserve neglected records | Contradictory evidence, incomplete interpretations | Record source uncertainty rather than proclaim hidden truth |

The Covenant's central warning becomes substantially correct, but its uncontrolled summons, fanatic violence, and civilian suffering remain morally consequential. The protagonist's early conflicts are genuine. Mother Vaska consistently opposes the flame's system while remaining accountable for harm inflicted by her followers.

## Player-facing authoring contract

Every discovery records its source, chapter, reliability, and text. Religious claims remain attributed teachings; an observation records what was actually observed. Contradictory later evidence appends to the earlier account instead of silently rewriting it. A damaged inscription is not automatically objective truth.

Chapter I runtime has a strict public lore registry: Nhalis, First Fire, Synod, silent ward, Covenant traces, shrine tending inscription, Veilglass Mirror, broken ritual, Warden, and small light restored. Unlocks follow conversations or actions. Developer truth stays in this document. Future content must extend discovery events with an explicit reveal chapter, never import this document or automatically expose all entries.
